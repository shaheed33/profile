Side project, OECD data

Who gives Maldives grants, and for what?

Grants to Maldives from every donor that reports to the OECD, and from China, mapped by the type of donor and the Sustainable Development Goal each grant mainly serves.

This is the companion to the map of loans. The Ministry of Finance register lists borrowing only, so the grants that many partners give are not in it. This page fills that gap from the OECD Creditor Reporting System, where donors report each year what they have committed and paid out, adds China's grants from AidData, and sets the grants alongside the loans. Amounts are in US dollars at the prices of each year.

For the loans, see Who lends to Maldives, and for what?

Which goals do the grants serve?

Donors report the sector of every grant using an OECD purpose code, such as basic drinking water or solar energy. Each code is matched to the goal it serves most directly, and each grant is counted once. Select a goal to list the donors behind it.

Grants and loans over time

Grants are what donors committed in each period. Loans are those signed in the same period and still in the Ministry of Finance register. Loans that have been fully repaid are not in the register, so loans in earlier periods are understated, and the two should be compared with that in mind.

What is under way now

The OECD figures above run a year or two behind. This list adds activities that organisations report as current to the International Aid Transparency Initiative (IATI). IATI mixes grants and loans, and some lines are trade finance or investment in private companies, so they are labelled and shown separately. Amounts here are not added to the totals above, because many of the same projects are already counted there. Each title links to the activity on d-portal.

How complete is this?

This page brings together the main published sources, but some grants are not recorded in any of them.

How this was made

Source and date

The OECD Creditor Reporting System, which records official development finance reported by donors for each recipient, retrieved through the OECD Data Explorer in October 2026.

China's grants

China does not report to the OECD. Its grants come from AidData's Global Chinese Development Finance Dataset, version 3.0, which records Chinese official finance from 2000 to 2021 using official and media sources. Only projects AidData recommends for totals are used, at their value when committed. AidData records commitments only, so the table shows no disbursements for China. Its sector groups are matched to goals in the same way as the OECD codes, in an editable lookup file.

What counts as a grant

The figures are grants within official development assistance, as reported by each donor. They are commitments, the amount pledged in a year, unless the table says disbursed. They are in US dollars at the prices of each year, so they are not adjusted for inflation.

Loans on this page come from the Ministry of Finance register used for the map of loans, not from the OECD. The register also covers lenders that do not report to the OECD, and using one source for each avoids counting a loan twice. The OECD's own loan figures are used only for the chart of how each lender divides grants and loans.

How donors were grouped
  • Bilateral. Governments and their aid agencies, such as Japan, the United Arab Emirates, Australia and Saudi Arabia.
  • Multilateral. Development banks, EU institutions and funds owned by several countries, such as the World Bank's IDA, the Asian Development Bank, the Green Climate Fund and the Global Environment Facility.
  • UN agencies. UN funds, programmes and specialised agencies, such as UNDP, UNICEF, WHO, UNFPA and IFAD. IFAD is listed with the UN here, as the OECD lists it, and with the multilateral lenders on the map of loans.

The grouping is kept in an editable lookup file. Totals that the OECD reports for groups of donors are left out, so that each grant is counted once.

How goals were assigned

Each OECD purpose code is matched to the goal it serves most directly. Education codes point to SDG 4, health to SDG 3, water and sanitation to SDG 6, energy to SDG 7, transport and communications to SDG 9, fishing to SDG 14, and disaster risk reduction and environmental policy to SDG 13. Public finance management and statistics go to SDG 17, as on the map of loans. Budget and import support are grouped as general support, emergency relief as humanitarian, and grants that donors did not assign to a sector as unclassified.

Biodiversity grants are matched to SDG 14, life below water, because most biodiversity work in Maldives is marine. The full list is in an editable lookup file, and any code can be moved and the page rebuilt.

What this does not show
  • Donors that do not report to the OECD. China's grants are added from AidData up to 2021. India and some others are not included. See how complete this is, above.
  • Commitments, not money spent. A commitment can be paid out over several years, or not in full.
  • Not only to government. The figures include grants channelled through NGOs, UN agencies and others, not only those paid to the government.
  • Sectors, not results. A purpose code describes what a grant was for, not what it achieved.
  • Other sources. The International Aid Transparency Initiative publishes more recent, project level data through d-portal. Its totals mix grants, loans, trade finance and private investment, and overlap with the figures here, so it is used only for the list of current activities and never added to the totals.
  • Totals by donor and sector, not projects. The data used here adds up projects within each donor, sector and year, so individual projects are not listed.
  • Recent years. Donors report with a delay, so the latest year left out here holds only a few early reports.
Files

The tagged data, by year, donor and purpose, is free to download as a CSV file, and the page reads from this JSON file. A Python script reads the OECD download, groups the donors and assigns the goals, so the page can be rebuilt when the OECD publishes a new year. The list of current activities comes from a d-portal export, read by a second script into its own JSON file.
