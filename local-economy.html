Every council area in Australia

What makes your local economy tick?

Pick any council area to see what its residents do for a living, what it specialises in, where its jobs growth has come from, and how well placed it is to ride out a downturn or a change in a big industry. Every area is compared only with areas like it, using official ABS figures.

    How the score works, and its limits

    The rules this page follows
    1. Like with like. Each area is scored only against similar areas, grouped by whether most residents live in a capital city, how remote they are, how densely settled the area is and its size. A farming shire is never scored against an inner city suburb.
    2. Five separate parts. Resilience has no single official measure, so the score is built from five parts that economists commonly use. How spread out jobs are across industries, how many jobs are in industries exposed to commodity prices or the shift to net zero, jobs growth, business growth, and incomes. Each part is shown on its own.
    3. Scores are positions, not grades. A score of 70 means the area does better on that part than about 70% of similar areas. It does not mean 70% of some ideal.
    4. You choose what matters. The overall score weights the five parts equally unless you change the weights. The page also shows how far the result moves across hundreds of other reasonable weightings, so a result that depends on one choice of weights is easy to spot.
    5. Only parts that hold up. Every part is tested by moving its figures by a year and checking that areas keep much the same order. A part whose order changes a lot (a consistency score below 0.6 out of 1) is shown but left out of the overall score. All five parts currently pass.
    6. Small areas are treated with care. Areas whose residents hold fewer than 1,000 employee jobs are described but not scored, because a single business opening or closing can swing their figures. Changes over time are noisier than levels, so jobs growth and business growth are only scored for areas with at least 5,000 jobs. In smaller areas their order changes too much from one period to the next to be trusted ().
    7. Careful words. A large share of jobs in mining, manufacturing, energy or farming is shown as exposure to swings and change, not as a forecast of job losses. Many of these industries are growing.
    What each part measures
    Where the growth comes from, explained

    Jobs growth in an area is split into two parts, a method economists call shift-share analysis. The industry mix part is the growth the area would have had if each of its industries had grown at the Australian rate for that industry. An area full of fast-growing industries gets a boost from its mix even if nothing special happens locally. The local part is everything else, such as population growth, new investment or a business closing. It is worked out as actual growth minus expected growth.

    What these figures cannot tell you

    The jobs figures count jobs held by people who live in the area, wherever they work, because that is how the ABS publishes them. An area with a big hospital that most staff commute into will show fewer health jobs than the area that houses those staff. For most questions about how exposed residents are to a downturn, that is the right way round.

    The ABS counts every employee job held at any time during the year, including second jobs and short ones. Years with a lot of people changing jobs, such as 2022, show more jobs everywhere, which is why each area's growth is compared with Australia's and with similar areas rather than read on its own.

    The figures cover employee jobs. Owner-operators without employees, who are common in farming and trades, are not counted in the industry mix. Industries are broad divisions, so coal mining cannot be separated from other mining, and a coal-fired power station sits inside energy, water and waste with water utilities.

    Resilience depends on more than these five parts, including skills, infrastructure, distance to markets and local leadership. Treat the score as a starting point for questions, not a verdict.

    How the similar-area groups are made

    Each area is placed by where most of its residents live, using the ABS remoteness classification and the 2021 Census count for each of Australia's 368,000 mesh blocks, the smallest areas the ABS publishes.

    Sources

    ABS Jobs in Australia (employee jobs by industry and residence), ABS Counts of Australian Businesses, ABS Personal Income in Australia, ABS Estimated Resident Population, the 2021 Census time series on industry of employment, and the ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard, all through the ABS Data by Region service. Built with an open method that refreshes each month.
