    Open method

    How the numbers work

    What the starting point is

    Each government's own 2026-27 budget, for the , as published between May and June 2026. The figures come from the tables every Australian government publishes under the , so they use the same definitions. The tool repeats the published path for 2026-27 to 2029-30 exactly when every shock is zero; a test checks this.

    2025-26 figures are the from the same papers, used for net debt at the start of the scenario.

    Exactly how a shock is calculated

    For each year from 2026-27 (year 1) to 2029-30 (year 4), with every amount taken from that year's published budget:

    • Revenue shocks change that year's published GST, other grants, payroll tax, stamp duty or royalties by the percentage chosen. It is a lasting change in level, not repeated growth.
    • Wages: extra cost = published employee and superannuation expenses × [(1 + extra growth)year − 1].
    • Other running costs: extra cost = (total expenses − employee costs − interest) × the percentage chosen.
    • Infrastructure: extra spending = published purchases of non-financial assets × the percentage chosen. It is paid for with debt and does not reduce the operating balance directly.
    • Interest: any extra debt the scenario creates costs the borrowing rate plus the rate shock, with half a year's interest in the year it is raised. The rate shock also reaches debt already in the budget as it is refinanced: the share refinanced each year × the year number, up to 100%, × net debt at the start of the year.
    • Operating balance = published balance + revenue change − extra wages − extra running costs − extra interest.
    • Net debt = published net debt + the running total of (fall in the operating balance + extra infrastructure spending).

    Depreciation, asset sales, the timing difference between accounting and cash, and any response by the government (raising taxes, cutting spending) are left out. Governments would respond; the point is to show the size of the gap they would need to close.

    What this tool cannot tell you

    It is not a forecast. It attaches no probability to any scenario and draws no confidence bands. It does not model how shocks feed into each other (a recession that cuts payroll tax also usually cuts stamp duty and GST; the "mild downturn" scenario combines them for that reason). Commonwealth decisions, such as GST top-up payments to Western Australia, are as published in the budgets.

    Accounting rules still differ a little between governments, for example in how superannuation investments and lease liabilities are treated. Where a government's headline figure uses a different definition, the tool uses the common definition and says so in the notes for that government.

    Research next: can trouble be spotted earlier?

    The research question behind this tool: which governments are most exposed to plausible shocks, and which indicators help explain later budget deterioration? Eight governments are too few for machine learning. The next step is a transparent backtest: take each budget from the last two decades, apply what actually happened to the economy, and compare the result with the final outcome. That needs historical budget papers checked by publication date, which are not yet assembled. Until then, the tool gives no risk scores or rankings beyond the arithmetic above.
