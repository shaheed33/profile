How the clock works

Every number on the Maldives National Debt Clock comes from published official statistics. This page explains where each one comes from, how it is calculated, and what it leaves out. Figures in the shaded boxes are live, worked out from the same data the clock uses.

1. Where the data comes from

All economic figures come from the Maldives Monetary Authority (MMA) Statistics Database, which publishes data compiled by the MMA, the Ministry of Finance and Planning, the Maldives Bureau of Statistics (MBS), the Maldives Customs Service and the World Bank.

Every morning at 08:00 Malé time, an automated script downloads the latest figures through the MMA's official API and saves them to a file the website reads. Nothing is typed in by hand, with one exception, the population estimate, explained in section 6. The code is public, so anyone can check exactly what it does: github.com/shaheed33/maldives-debt-clock.

This site is independent. It is not produced or endorsed by the MMA, the Ministry of Finance or any government body.

2. What counts as debt

The clock shows public and publicly guaranteed debt of the central government (MMA series 4514), measured in rufiyaa at the end of each quarter. It is made up of three parts.

  • Domestic debt is money the government has borrowed inside the Maldives, such as treasury bills, treasury bonds and loans from local banks and the MMA.
  • External debt is money borrowed from abroad, including loans from other governments, development banks and commercial lenders, and international bonds and sukuk.
  • Guaranteed debt is loans taken by state-owned companies where the government has promised to repay if the company can't. These are counted in full, even though the companies are expected to repay them themselves.

It does not include borrowing by state-owned companies that the government has not guaranteed, or the debts of private businesses and households.

3. The live clock

The government's debt is officially measured once a quarter, and published some weeks after the quarter ends. A clock that only changed four times a year wouldn't tell you much, so between releases the clock estimates where the debt is now, by assuming it keeps growing at the pace of the past year.

Why the past four quarters

Using a full year evens out swings within the year, such as a large bond repayment or a burst of borrowing in one quarter, while still reflecting the government's recent pace rather than its long-term average. The trade-off is that a sudden change in borrowing only shows up in the clock once the next official figure is published. If debt fell over the past year, the clock counts down instead of up.

When a new quarter is published, the clock resets to the new official figure and recalculates the pace. That reset can make the number jump up or down.

4. Debt as a share of GDP

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of everything produced in the Maldives in a year. Comparing debt with GDP shows how heavy the debt is relative to the size of the economy. It is the measure economists and lenders watch most closely. On the front page, the sea surface marks the point where debt equals a full year of GDP (100%). When debt is higher than that, the yellow line sits below the surface, and the deeper it is, the further the country is "underwater" on its debt. The gauge on the right reads 100% at the surface and counts down into the water.

The ratio is published by the MMA (series 4522) as total debt divided by nominal GDP for that year. We use their figure directly, multiplied by 100 because the MMA publishes it as a decimal (for example, 1.227 rather than 122.7%).

Earlier years use actual GDP as revised by the MBS on 1 September 2026. If the forecast turns out too high or too low, the ratio will be revised.

Domestic and external debt as shares of GDP use MMA series 4523 and 4526 in the same way.

5. Who the debt is owed to

The breakdown uses the MMA's split of total debt into four parts. These are domestic debt of the central government (4516), domestic guaranteed debt (4517), external debt of the central government (4519) and external guaranteed debt (4520). Each share is that part divided by the total, for the latest quarter.

The front page also reports how each part changed over the latest quarter, simply the latest figure minus the one before.

"Grown X times since 2015" is the latest total divided by the first quarter in the MMA series (March 2015). This compares rufiyaa amounts directly and does not adjust for inflation or for the economy growing. The debt-to-GDP ratio is the better measure for that.

6. Population and per-citizen figures

Per-citizen figures divide a total, such as the debt, by the number of Maldivian citizens. This turns a number too large to picture into something easier to grasp. It is a way of showing scale, not a bill. No individual owes this amount.

Why citizens, not everyone living here

About one in four people living in the Maldives is a foreign worker (132,493 at the 2022 census). Foreign residents do pay some taxes, but the debt is owed by the Maldivian state, and repaying it will fall on Maldivian taxpayers and public services over many years. So we divide by Maldivian citizens. Dividing by everyone living here would make each per-person figure roughly a quarter smaller.

At first sight the jump looks too big. Going from 382,751 to 415,294 is 32,543 more people in a little over three years, about 2.5% a year. That is nearly twice the 1.3% a year the Maldivian population grew between the 2014 and 2022 censuses. But the two sources measure different things. The census counts people who are in the country, so it misses Maldivians studying or working abroad. The register counts every citizen. A register can also run high if deaths aren't removed from it, so we checked for that.

Checking the register against the census

We took everyone counted in the 2022 census by age, added three years to each person's age, and compared the result with the 2025 register. If the two sources agreed perfectly and nobody died, every row would match. Children aged 0 to 2 were born after the census, so they appear only in the register.

This shows three things.

  • The register is about 4% higher at almost every age from 3 to 69. An even gap across ages is what you would expect from Maldivians living abroad plus people the census missed. An error, such as duplicate records, would more likely be concentrated in particular ages.
  • At 70 and over, the register is lower. That is what should happen if deaths are being removed from it. If they weren't, the register would show a build-up of elderly people instead. The fall is a little smaller than expected deaths at those ages, so a small number of deaths may not yet be recorded, but it is not large enough to matter here.
  • Births look right. The register has 17,582 children aged 0 to 2, about 5,900 a year. The census found about 5,800 a year among children under five.

In short, 415,294 is a credible count of all Maldivian citizens at the end of 2025. Of the 32,543 gap with the census, roughly half is real growth since 2022 (births minus deaths). The other half is the difference in what the two sources count, namely citizens abroad and people the census did not reach.

Estimating today's population

The register figure is for the end of 2025. Why we don't use the MMA population series

An earlier version of this site used MMA series 79, the MBS population projection. We stopped because it doesn't fit this purpose, for three reasons.

  • It includes foreign residents, not just citizens.
  • From 2015 to 2022 it grows by exactly 2.8% every year, which suggests an old projection that was never updated with real data. For 2022 it gives 547,651 people, while the census actually counted 515,132.
  • It then jumps 7.2% in a single year, from 581,053 in 2025 to 623,138 in 2026.

7. Revenue and spending

Revenue is total revenue and grants (MMA series 1999). It covers taxes, fees and charges, income from state assets, capital receipts and foreign grants. Spending is total recurrent and capital expenditure (series 2034). It covers wages, running costs, subsidies, interest and investment.

Both are published monthly. A single month can be misleading, since taxes are often paid in particular months. So we add up the most recent 12 months, giving a full year that moves forward each time a new month is published. If revenue and spending are published up to different months, we stop at the latest month both have.

Two things to keep in mind.

  • Monthly government finance figures are preliminary and can be revised later.
  • Spending here does not include repaying the principal on existing loans (that appears separately, in series 2076). So the gap between spending and revenue is smaller than the total amount the government needs to borrow each year.

Why the yearly gap is much smaller than the debt

The gap between spending and revenue is one year's shortfall, while the debt has built up over many years. The front page shows how the rise in debt since the start of the monthly data (January 2017) breaks down. It adds up the monthly gaps between spending and revenue, then adds the change in government-guaranteed debt of state-owned companies, which isn't government spending. Whatever remains is other borrowing that isn't counted as spending in series 2034. That includes lending to and investing in state-owned companies (which the MMA lists separately, as memorandum items) and changes in the government's cash balances. The remainder is worked out by subtraction, so it also absorbs any differences in timing or coverage between the monthly and quarterly data.

Where a year's spending goes

The spending breakdown uses four MMA sub-series. These are salaries, wages and pensions (2036); grants, contributions and subsidies (2050); financing and interest costs (2049); and capital spending (2057). Each is shown as a share of total spending over the same 12 months. They don't add up to 100%, because the rest is other running costs, such as supplies, services, travel and maintenance.

9. US dollar figures

The MMA records debt in rufiyaa. When you switch to US dollars, figures are converted using the MMA's reference rate (series 4039, rufiyaa per US dollar, end of month). Current figures use the latest rate. Each past quarter on the chart uses the rate for that month.

Rate in use

… per US dollar

Much of the external debt was originally borrowed in dollars or other currencies and converted to rufiyaa by the Ministry of Finance. Our dollar figures convert the rufiyaa totals back, so they will not exactly match the original currency amounts.

10. Adjusting for inflation

Prices rise over time, so a rufiyaa in 2015 bought more than a rufiyaa today. The Real button at the top of the page restates past amounts in recent prices, so years can be compared fairly. Nominal shows amounts as they were actually recorded.

We use the national consumer price index (CPI, MMA series 280), published monthly by the Maldives Bureau of Statistics. Rather than one month, the base is the average CPI of the latest 12 months. The CPI can move a lot from one month to the next (it rose 3.8% in April 2026 alone), so a single-month base would make every adjusted figure jump around.

What Real changes, and what it doesn't

Real applies only to comparisons over time, meaning the debt chart, the "grown X times" comparison and the year-by-year revenue and spending chart. For yearly totals, each month is adjusted before adding up.

Current figures are always in today's money, whichever button is selected. That covers the clock, debt per citizen, the latest breakdown, the latest quarter's changes, and the last 12 months of revenue, spending and interest. Adjusting very recent amounts would only nudge them up or down by the month-to-month movement in prices, which would be confusing rather than useful.

Changing the base period changes the adjusted amounts, but not the comparisons. "Debt grew 3 times after inflation" is the same whichever base is used. The debt-to-GDP ratio isn't affected, because debt and GDP are measured in the same year's prices. In US dollar mode, real amounts are converted at today's exchange rate.

11. Fuel imports and crises

The fuel imports page shows the monthly value of the Maldives' petroleum imports recorded by the Maldives Customs Service (MMA series 3503), split into petrol (3504), diesel (3505) and other products (3507). The share of all imports uses total goods imports (3486). The world oil price line is the World Bank's monthly average of Brent, Dubai and WTI crude prices (3787), in US dollars a barrel.

Why the fuel page uses US dollars

Fuel is bought and recorded in US dollars, so the charts, yearly totals and crisis comparisons stay in dollars. Converting the history to rufiyaa would distort it. The rufiyaa was fixed at 12.80 per US dollar from July 2001 to March 2011, then moved to about 15.40 from April 2011. In rufiyaa, every bill after 2011 would look about 20% bigger relative to earlier years, for reasons that have nothing to do with fuel. Only the headline figures at the top of the page follow the MVR/US$ switch, converted at each month's MMA reference rate. The Real button isn't offered on this page, because Maldivian consumer prices aren't the right way to adjust prices set in US dollars.

Series break in April 2015

According to the MMA's note, after amendments to the Export Import Act reinstated duty on petroleum products from 1 April 2015, the series only includes the value of fuel sold within the Maldives. Values before and after are not fully comparable, which is why the chart marks this date.

Highest and lowest months

The chart marks the highest and lowest monthly values in whichever period is selected. These are simply the largest and smallest values in the data; they aren't adjusted for prices or the size of the economy.

How crisis periods are chosen

Each shaded period is a well-documented event that moved world oil markets or the Maldivian economy. War and conflict periods are shown in red, and other crises in amber. The start of each period is the month the event began. The end is our judgement of when its main effect on oil prices passed, so the exact edges are approximate.

12. Time and updates

The time shown on the clock is Malé time (UTC+5), whatever time zone the visitor is in.

The site checks the MMA for new data every morning at 08:00 Malé time. The MMA publishes debt figures quarterly and government finance figures monthly, so most days nothing changes. The footer shows when data was last fetched.

If the data stops updating

The latest data is saved alongside the website, so the site keeps working even if the MMA's data service is unavailable. The clock keeps estimating from the last official figure. To make sure that isn't misleading, the site shows a warning at the top of every page in any of these cases.

  • the latest official debt figure is more than seven months old (normally a new quarter is published within about three months);
  • the data hasn't been refreshed for more than eight days; or
  • some figures couldn't be updated at the last refresh. In that case the previous values are kept rather than dropped, and the warning says so.

13. Limitations

  • The clock between quarterly releases is an estimate based on the past year's pace, not a live record of borrowing.
  • The current debt-to-GDP ratio relies on a GDP forecast.
  • Monthly revenue and spending figures are preliminary.
  • The population is an estimate built from the register and past census growth.
  • Guaranteed debt is counted in full, although state companies are expected to repay most of it themselves.
  • In nominal mode, comparisons over time are not adjusted for inflation. Switch to Real to adjust them.
  • Crisis periods on the fuel page are approximate, and the before-and-after comparisons don't separate a crisis's effect from other causes.
