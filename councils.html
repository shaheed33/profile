Official council figures

How does your council compare?

Councils collect bins, decide planning permits, look after local roads and run libraries. Both Victoria and New South Wales make every council report the same figures each year. This page lines your council up against councils like it, never against the whole state, so a small country shire is not judged against inner Melbourne or Sydney.

How this is kept fair

The rules this page follows
  1. Every council is compared only with its official peer group, the groups each state government uses itself. Victoria and NSW are never compared with each other, because they measure different things in different ways.
  2. There is no overall score and no league table. Each measure stands on its own, so a council's strengths and weaknesses both show.
  3. Figures are averaged over up to three years, so one unusual year does not decide the result. Where a council's own figures swing much more than the gap between councils, the page says so.
  4. A council is called better or weaker than most only when it sits in the top or bottom quarter of its group. Everything in between is typical.
  5. Some measures have no right answer. Spending more on libraries can mean better service, not waste. Those are shown without a verdict.
  6. Measures where councils' positions jump around from year to year are left out, as they say more about one-off events or accounting timing than about performance. Any year whose figures look mislabelled or out of range is left out too.
  7. In Victoria, each council's own explanation of its result is shown next to it, in its own words.
What these figures cannot tell you

They describe what councils report, not everything they do. Results reflect things councils do not control, such as the size and spread of the area, how fast it is growing, the age and income of residents, and the grants it receives. A council can also choose to spend more to provide more. The context shown under each council's name is there to keep this in view.
