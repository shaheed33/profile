Official council figures

How does your council compare?

Councils collect bins, decide planning permits, look after local roads and run libraries. Victoria and New South Wales make every council report the same figures each year. This page uses only the figures that hold up to testing, and lines your council up against councils like it, never against the whole state. Pick a second council to compare the two side by side.

How this is kept fair

The rules this page follows
  1. Like with like. Every council is compared only with its official peer group, the groups each state government uses itself. Victoria and NSW are never compared with each other, because they measure different things in different ways.
  2. No overall score. Each measure stands on its own. The report card shows where a council sits on each one, and the measures are not equally important, so the squares should not be added up into a score.
  3. Several years, not one. Figures are averaged over up to three years so one unusual year does not decide the result, and each year is shown. Where a council's own figures swing much more than the gap between councils, the page says so.
  4. Only clear differences count. A council is called better or weaker than most only when it sits in the top or bottom quarter of its group. In a two-council comparison, gaps smaller than a tenth of the usual spread between councils are called about the same. For residents' survey scores, which come from a few hundred residents per council, gaps under 4 points are within the margin of error and are also called about the same.
  5. Cost is not the same as quality. Measures of what a service costs to run are labelled cheaper or costlier to run, not better or worse, because a cheaper service can be a thinner one.
  6. Some measures have no right answer. Rates, spending per resident and similar figures are shown with the reason, but without a verdict.
  7. No winner across groups. When two councils from different groups are compared, the page shows both figures and the difference, but does not name a winner.
  8. Only reliable measures. Every official measure is tested before it is used. It is left out if councils' positions change a lot from one year to the next (a consistency score below 0.6 out of 1), if so many councils report exactly the same figure that it cannot separate them, if each council defines it differently, or if it reflects choices or circumstances rather than performance. A year whose figures look mislabelled is left out too. The full list is below.
  9. The council's side. In Victoria, each council's own explanation of its result is shown next to it, in its own words.
Measures not included, and why
What these figures cannot tell you

The figures are reported by the councils themselves. Victoria's are checked by the state and partly audited, and NSW's financial figures come from audited statements, but definitions can still be applied a little differently from one council to the next.

They describe what councils report, not everything they do. Results also reflect things councils do not control, such as the size and spread of the area, how fast it is growing, the age and income of residents, and the grants it receives. The context under each council's name is there to keep this in view. A good result here is a reason to ask how, and a weak one a reason to ask why, not a final judgement.
